Understanding neuroblastoma

Neuroblastoma is a childhood cancer that begins in developing cells of the sympathetic nervous system. This guide explains how those cells normally develop, what can go wrong, and how the disease can affect a child's body.

01 · The basics

What is neuroblastoma?

Our bodies are made of tiny building blocks called cells. Neuroblastoma happens when some early, developing cells in the nervous system do not mature as they should and begin growing out of control.

Neuroblastoma is a tumour arising from immature cells of the sympathoadrenal lineage, which normally contributes to sympathetic neurons and adrenal medulla chromaffin cells. It is biologically diverse, and the molecular features vary between tumours.

Where does it begin?

It usually starts in the developing sympathetic nervous system. This system helps control automatic body functions, such as heart rate and digestion. Many neuroblastomas begin in the adrenal glands, which sit above the kidneys, or along nerve tissue near the spine.

The sympathetic nervous system is part of the autonomic nervous system. Neuroblastoma most commonly arises in the adrenal medulla or sympathetic ganglia along the paraspinal region.

02 · Normal development

How do these cells normally develop?

Before birth, a group of early cells called neural crest cells can develop into several different cell types. Some become part of the sympathetic nervous system.

Neural crest cells give rise to sympathoadrenal progenitors. These progenitors can differentiate along sympathetic neuronal or chromaffin cell lineages.

What helps guide development?

Cells use instructions and signals to know when to grow, when to survive, and when to mature into their proper role.

Several genes and signalling pathways help regulate sympathetic-lineage development. PHOX2B supports autonomic and sympathetic neuronal identity. MYCN helps support growth in early developing cells and is normally reduced as cells mature. ALK signalling contributes to normal cell survival and development.

  • PHOX2B: helps guide cells towards the correct nerve-cell identity.
  • MYCN: supports growth in early cells; its activity normally decreases as cells mature.
  • ALK: helps send signals involved in cell survival and development.
03 · What changes

What goes wrong in neuroblastoma?

Sometimes the normal instructions that guide a developing cell become disrupted. A cell may stay immature instead of finishing its development. It may also keep receiving signals that help it survive and divide.

Neuroblastoma is associated with disrupted differentiation and abnormal control of cell survival and proliferation. Some tumours contain specific molecular alterations, including MYCN amplification or activating ALK mutations. These alterations are not present in every case.

Typical development

A cell matures

The cell develops into a specialised cell, and its growth becomes controlled.

Differentiation proceeds and proliferative activity generally decreases as cells mature.

Neuroblastoma

A cell remains immature

Some cells do not mature properly and continue to survive and multiply.

Differentiation may be impaired, while pro-growth and pro-survival signals support continued proliferation and accumulation of abnormal cells.

Examples of genetic changes

  • MYCN amplification: extra copies of the MYCN gene can drive excessive growth and cell division.
  • Activating ALK mutations: certain changes can keep ALK growth and survival signalling switched on.

These are examples of alterations associated with some neuroblastomas, not a checklist that applies to every child. PHOX2B has an important role in normal developmental identity; it should not be assumed to be altered in every tumour.

Important: Neuroblastoma is not exactly the same in every child. Different tumours can have different genetic features and behave differently.
04 · From cells to a tumour

How can this lead to a tumour?

When abnormal cells keep surviving and dividing, they can build up in one place and form a mass called a tumour.

A combination of impaired differentiation, increased proliferation and enhanced survival can allow immature tumour cells to accumulate and form a neuroblastoma mass.

Immature cell→Does not mature normally→Survives and divides→Cells accumulate→Tumour
05 · Effects on the body

How can neuroblastoma affect a child?

The effects depend on where the tumour is, how large it becomes, and whether it has spread to other parts of the body.

Clinical effects reflect the primary tumour's location and local mass effect, as well as metastatic involvement. Bone marrow involvement can disrupt normal blood-cell production.

◉

Growth in one area

A tumour can press on nearby organs or tissues. In the abdomen, this may cause swelling, discomfort or pain.

Local mass effect may cause abdominal distension, pain or symptoms related to compression of nearby structures.

⌁

Spread to the bones

If cancer cells spread to bones, they can cause bone pain and damage.

Osseous metastases may cause bone pain and skeletal complications.

＋

Spread to bone marrow

Bone marrow makes blood cells. If cancer cells affect it, the body may make fewer healthy blood cells, which can contribute to tiredness and other problems.

Bone marrow infiltration can impair haematopoiesis, potentially reducing red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

Children can have different symptoms. These examples explain possible effects and are not a way to diagnose neuroblastoma.

06 · Helpful words

Quick glossary

Cell differentiation

The process by which an early cell develops into a more specialised cell with a particular job.

Proliferation

An increase in cell number through cell division.

Neural crest cells

Early embryonic cells that can develop into several different cell types, including cells in parts of the nervous system.

Sympathetic nervous system

A part of the autonomic nervous system that helps the body respond to changes, including by influencing heart rate and other automatic functions.

Gene amplification

An increase in the number of copies of a particular gene. Extra copies can sometimes increase the gene's activity.

Mutation

A change in the DNA sequence of a gene.

07 · Remember

The key message

Neuroblastoma begins in early developing cells that do not mature normally. These cells can continue to survive and multiply, forming a tumour that may affect nearby tissues or spread to other parts of the body.

Neuroblastoma is a biologically diverse childhood cancer arising from the sympathoadrenal lineage. Disrupted differentiation and altered survival and proliferation pathways contribute to tumour development, while clinical effects depend on tumour location and disease spread.
