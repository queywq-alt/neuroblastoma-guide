Understanding neuroblastoma

Neuroblastoma is a cancer that begins in early developing cells that are meant to become part of the body's automatic nervous system. This guide explores how those cells normally grow, what can go wrong, and how the disease may affect a child.

Neuroblastoma is an embryonal malignancy arising from the sympathoadrenal lineage of the neural crest. Its biology is heterogeneous, with tumour behaviour influenced by developmental state, genomic alterations and clinical context.

01 · The basics

What is neuroblastoma?

Our bodies are made of tiny building blocks called cells. Before birth, some cells are still learning what their job will be. Neuroblastoma can develop when cells from one of these early cell groups do not mature normally and begin to grow in an uncontrolled way.

Neuroblastoma is a paediatric malignancy arising from immature cells of the sympathoadrenal lineage. This lineage normally gives rise to sympathetic neurons and adrenal medulla chromaffin cells. Neuroblastoma is biologically heterogeneous; its molecular features and clinical behaviour vary between tumours.

Neuroblastoma often begins in the adrenal glands, which sit above the kidneys, or in sympathetic nerve tissue near the spine.
Primary sites commonly include the adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia in paraspinal or other sympathetic-chain locations. The drawing is schematic and not anatomically to scale.
02 · Normal development

How do these cells normally develop?

Before birth, neural crest cells are early cells that can develop into several different cell types. Some become sympathoadrenal progenitor cells. These can then mature along different paths, including into sympathetic nerve cells or adrenal cells.

Neural crest-derived sympathoadrenal progenitors undergo lineage specification and differentiation. They can give rise to sympathetic neuronal cells or chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. This is a simplified overview of a complex developmental process.

Simplified developmental pathway. The two branches represent distinct cell fates rather than a single linear sequence.
Pathway A

Sympathetic neuron

A specialised nerve cell that helps send signals involved in automatic body functions.

A differentiated sympathetic neuron contributes to autonomic signalling and regulation of involuntary physiological functions.

Pathway B

Chromaffin cell

A specialised cell in the inner part of the adrenal gland. It releases hormones such as adrenaline.

Adrenal medulla chromaffin cells are neuroendocrine cells that release catecholamines, including adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine).

What helps guide normal development?

Genes act like instructions that help cells develop their identity, grow at the right time and respond to survival signals. Three examples involved in this process are PHOX2B, MYCN and ALK.

Sympathoadrenal development is regulated by transcription factors and signalling pathways. PHOX2B is a key transcriptional regulator of autonomic neuronal development; MYCN is a growth-regulating transcription factor; and ALK is a receptor tyrosine kinase involved in developmental signalling and cell survival.

PHOX2B
Helps guide developing cells towards the correct nerve-cell identity.
Homeobox transcription factor important for autonomic neuronal specification and differentiation.
MYCN
Helps early cells grow. Its activity is normally reduced as cells mature.
MYCN encodes a transcription factor that regulates genes involved in cell growth, proliferation and differentiation; its expression is developmentally regulated.
ALK
Provides signals that help regulate cell growth and survival.
Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) is a receptor tyrosine kinase that mediates intracellular signalling involved in neuronal development and cell survival.
03 · What changes

What can go wrong in neuroblastoma?

Sometimes the processes that guide a developing cell become disrupted. Instead of maturing into a specialised cell, it may remain immature. Changes that affect growth or survival can also help abnormal cells continue to multiply.

Neuroblastoma development is associated with impaired differentiation and dysregulation of cell proliferation and survival. The relative contribution of these processes differs between tumours; there is no single molecular sequence that explains every case.

Typical development

A cell matures

The cell becomes more specialised and its growth is regulated.

Differentiation proceeds and proliferative activity generally decreases as the cell acquires a specialised phenotype.

Neuroblastoma

A cell remains immature

Some cells do not mature normally. They may continue to survive and divide, allowing abnormal cells to build up.

Impaired differentiation, together with dysregulated pro-survival and proliferative signalling, can contribute to the accumulation of immature tumour cells.

Conceptual comparison: neuroblastoma is not caused by one identical sequence of events in every child.

Examples of molecular alterations

Some neuroblastomas have changes in genes that affect how cells grow or survive. Two examples are extra copies of MYCN or certain changes in ALK. These changes are not found in every child's tumour.

Examples of recurrent genomic alterations include MYCN amplification, which is associated with aggressive tumour biology, and activating ALK mutations, which can enhance receptor tyrosine kinase signalling. Neither alteration is present in all neuroblastomas, and additional molecular subtypes and drivers exist.

MYCN amplification

The cell has extra copies of the MYCN gene. This can increase growth-related activity and encourage cells to keep dividing.

Increased MYCN gene copy number can lead to MYCN overexpression and dysregulation of transcriptional programmes controlling proliferation, differentiation and cell survival.

Activating ALK mutations

Certain changes in ALK can make growth and survival signals more active than they should be.

Activating ALK mutations can produce constitutive or enhanced kinase signalling through downstream pathways that regulate proliferation and survival.

Important: Neuroblastoma is different from child to child. These are examples of changes that can contribute to some tumours, not a checklist that applies to every child. PHOX2B has an important normal developmental role; it should not be assumed to be altered in every tumour.
04 · The molecular picture

How do genes fit into the story?

Think of a developing cell as following a set of instructions. Some instructions help it learn its role, some help it grow, and some help it respond to signals that support survival. When parts of this control system are disrupted, a cell may not develop as expected.

The molecular biology of neuroblastoma involves interacting developmental transcriptional programmes and signalling networks. PHOX2B, MYCN and ALK are useful examples for understanding lineage identity, proliferation and survival, but they do not represent all molecular drivers or alterations in the disease.

These are simplified functional associations, not a complete map of the pathways or a claim that all three genes are altered in every tumour.
05 · From cells to a tumour

How can abnormal cells form a tumour?

When abnormal cells continue to survive and divide, they can build up in one area. Over time, this collection of cells can form a mass called a tumour.

Impaired differentiation, dysregulated proliferation and altered survival can contribute to the accumulation of immature tumour cells and formation of a primary neuroblastoma mass. The contribution of each process varies between tumours.

06 · Effects on the body

How can neuroblastoma affect a child?

The effects depend on where the tumour starts, how large it becomes and whether it has spread. Select an area on the illustration to explore some possible effects.

Clinical manifestations depend on the primary tumour site, local mass effect and metastatic distribution. Common sites of metastatic disease include bone and bone marrow; marrow infiltration can impair normal haematopoiesis.

Select a highlighted area or use the buttons.

Growth in one area

A tumour in the abdomen can press on nearby organs and tissues. This may cause a swollen tummy, discomfort or pain.

A primary abdominal or adrenal mass may cause local mass effect, abdominal distension, pain or symptoms related to compression of adjacent structures.

These are possible effects, not symptoms every child will experience. A child's healthcare team can explain what applies to their individual situation.

The manifestations listed are examples and are not diagnostic criteria. Individual presentation depends on disease site, extent and other clinical factors.

07 · Helpful words

Quick glossary

The glossary defines selected developmental and oncological terms used throughout this guide.

Cell differentiation

The process by which an early cell develops into a more specialised cell with a particular job.

The process by which a less specialised cell acquires the structural and functional characteristics of a more specialised cell.

Proliferation

An increase in the number of cells through cell division.

An increase in cell number resulting from cell-cycle progression and mitosis.

Neural crest cells

Early cells in an embryo that can develop into several different cell types, including parts of the nervous system.

A transient, multipotent embryonic cell population that migrates and differentiates into diverse derivatives, including components of the peripheral nervous system.

Sympathoadrenal progenitor

An early developing cell that can become part of the sympathetic nerves or the adrenal medulla.

A neural crest-derived progenitor population that gives rise to sympathetic neuronal and chromaffin lineages.

Gene amplification

When a cell has extra copies of a particular gene. This can sometimes increase the amount of activity linked to that gene.

An increase in genomic copy number of a gene; in neuroblastoma, MYCN amplification is a recognised adverse biological feature.

Mutation

A change in the DNA sequence.

A permanent alteration in the nucleotide sequence of the genome. The functional effect depends on the specific variant and its context.

Metastasis

When cancer cells spread from where the cancer first started to another part of the body.

The dissemination and establishment of malignant cells at sites distant from the primary tumour.

Haematopoiesis

The process by which the body makes blood cells, mainly in the bone marrow.

The production and development of blood cells from haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells.

08 · Remember

The key message

Neuroblastoma begins in early developing cells that do not mature normally. These cells can continue to survive and multiply, forming a tumour that may press on nearby tissues or spread to other parts of the body. Every child's experience can be different.

Neuroblastoma is a heterogeneous childhood malignancy of sympathoadrenal lineage. Disrupted differentiation and altered proliferative and survival signalling contribute to tumour development, while clinical effects depend on the primary site and extent of metastatic disease.
